# Chapter 4: An Unexpected Conversation

Friday afternoon found Doug in his car at 6:15 PM, sitting in the ShopStream parking lot with the engine off, too exhausted to drive home. He'd spent the entire day in what felt like a pointless cycle of meetings and interruptions, and he had nothing to show for it except a pounding headache and a growing sense of futility.

Maya had been shadowing different teams all week, appearing in meetings, taking notes, asking occasional questions that seemed simple but somehow made everyone think harder about what they were doing. Doug had seen her in the morning standup, watched her observe code reviews, noticed her sitting quietly in the corner during the afternoon incident response call when the staging environment had mysteriously died.

She never offered solutions. She just watched and asked questions.

Doug pulled out his phone and scrolled through his email. The Q4 planning doc was still unfinished. He had eleven performance reviews to write. The architecture proposal Raj had sent him was forty-seven pages and probably needed his feedback by Monday. His task list had grown from forty-three items to sixty-one.

He was going backwards.

A knock on his car window made him jump. Maya was standing there, holding her laptop bag, looking at him with concern.

Doug rolled down the window. "Dr. Patel. Sorry, I was just... taking a minute."

"Maya," she said. "And you don't need to apologize. Mind if I join you?"

Before Doug could answer, she was walking around to the passenger side and sliding into the seat. For a moment, they sat in silence, two people in a parked car in an emptying lot, not saying anything.

"Long week," Maya finally said.

"Is that a question or an observation?"

"Observation. I've been watching you, Doug. You and your team. Everyone here, really."

Doug waited for the critique, the list of things they were doing wrong, the consultant's recommendations for improving efficiency.

"Can I ask you something?" Maya said instead.

"Everyone keeps asking me if they can ask me something and then they just ask it anyway," Doug said, then immediately regretted the snark. "Sorry. Yes. Ask away."

"What do you do here?"

Doug blinked. "What do I do? I'm a lead engineer. Engineering manager. I oversee three teams, about twenty people total."

"No," Maya said. "I know your title. I'm asking what you actually do. How do you spend your time?"

Doug thought about his week. "Meetings, mostly. Code reviews when I have time. Unblocking people. Answering questions. Putting out fires. Architecture decisions. Planning. Coordination."

"When was the last time you wrote code?"

Doug had to think about it. "Tuesday morning. For about forty-five minutes. Then I got pulled into an incident call."

"And before that?"

"Maybe a week before? There was that emergency fix for the payment processing bug."

"Do you miss it? Writing code?"

Doug surprised himself by answering honestly. "Every day. That's why I got into this field. I love building things. Solving problems. Creating something from nothing. But now..." He gestured vaguely at the office building. "Now I spend my time managing the system that prevents building."

"That's an interesting way to phrase it," Maya said. "Managing the system that prevents building."

"Is it wrong?"

"Is it?" Maya asked. "You tell me. What percentage of your time do you spend on value creation versus... everything else?"

"Value creation," Doug repeated. "I guess it depends on what you count. Is unblocking someone value creation?"

"Is it?"

"I don't know," Doug admitted. "Sometimes I think I'm helping. Other times I think I'm just a glorified traffic cop, routing questions to people who could probably figure it out themselves if they had time to think."

Maya nodded. "Tell me about your best day at ShopStream. Not your most productive day—your best day. The day you went home feeling energized instead of drained."

Doug had to reach back in his memory. "Maybe eighteen months ago? We were building the new recommendation engine. Small team, just four of us. We'd lock ourselves in a conference room for hours, whiteboarding, coding, testing ideas. It felt like making music, you know? Everyone in sync, building on each other's ideas. We shipped it in three weeks and it worked beautifully."

"What made that different from now?"

"Everything," Doug said. "We had focus. We had autonomy. We had a clear goal. We didn't have sixteen different stakeholders with sixteen different opinions. We didn't have a process that required forty-seven steps and three months to deploy a button."

"You didn't have bureaucracy," Maya said.

"I mean... yes. But some of that bureaucracy exists for good reasons. We've been burned before. We needed more structure."

"Did you need more structure?" Maya asked. "Or did you need better structure?"

Doug didn't have an answer for that.

"Can I share something I've observed this week?" Maya continued, and Doug noticed she was asking permission more than most people did. "You have brilliant engineers. Talented, dedicated people. But they're trapped in a system that makes it almost impossible to do their best work. And I don't think anyone designed this system intentionally. It just... accumulated."

"Death by a thousand reasonable choices," Doug said, remembering Keisha's phrase.

"Exactly. Every policy, every meeting, every approval process—someone added it for a reason. But nobody stepped back to look at the whole picture. And now you have a system where twelve percent of time goes to value creation and eighty-eight percent goes to coordination, waiting, and rework."

"You talked to Keisha."

"I talked to everyone," Maya said. "And the numbers are even worse in some teams. Doug, you can't hire your way out of this. Even if you doubled the engineering team, you'd just have twice as many people spending eighty-eight percent of their time on overhead."

Doug leaned his head back against the seat. "So what do we do?"

"That's not the right question yet."

"What is the right question?"

"What if the problem isn't your people, but your system?"

Doug sat with that for a moment. "I don't understand the difference."

"Right now, everyone is working incredibly hard," Maya said. "Long hours. Constant stress. Dedicated effort. But the system is designed—unintentionally—to waste that effort. To create friction instead of flow. To optimize for safety instead of value. What if instead of pushing people to work harder, you redesigned the system to multiply their effectiveness?"

"That sounds great in theory," Doug said. "But we're not Google. We don't have their resources, their talent, their—"

"Google had to figure it out too," Maya interrupted gently. "They weren't born knowing how to deploy thousands of times a day. Amazon wasn't always Amazon. These companies transformed by changing how they worked, not just how hard they worked."

"And you think we can do that in ninety days?"

"I think you can start," Maya said. "Look, Doug, I'm not here to sell you a miracle solution. But I've seen this pattern before. Organizations that succeed in the modern world don't just use better tools—they fundamentally rethink how humans and systems work together."

Doug turned to look at her. "You're talking about AI. The agentic stuff Tom mentioned."

Maya smiled slightly. "I'm talking about something bigger than AI. But yes, agentic AI is part of it. Imagine if instead of you being the bottleneck for every decision, you had intelligent agents that could handle routine tasks. Imagine if code reviews happened instantly instead of in three days. Imagine if testing was continuous and automatic. Imagine if deployments were safe enough to happen twenty times a day instead of once a week."

"That sounds like science fiction."

"Five years ago, it would have been," Maya agreed. "But the technology exists now. The companies that figure out how to leverage it—not just as tools, but as colleagues—they're going to have an overwhelming advantage."

Doug thought about the feature timeline he'd mapped out. Seventy-eight days, eight days of coding. "And you think this could help us?"

"I think it could transform you," Maya said. "But only if you're willing to see the problem differently. Right now, you're trying to optimize the existing system. Make meetings more efficient. Speed up code reviews. Streamline processes. But you're still thinking inside the current paradigm."

"And the new paradigm is...?"

"Flow," Maya said simply. "Optimize for flow. Remove waste. Eliminate waiting. Enable teams to move from idea to production as quickly and safely as possible. And leverage agents—both human and AI—to multiply capability instead of adding overhead."

Doug's phone buzzed. A text from Michelle: "When are you coming home?"

He looked at the time. 6:47 PM. Another evening where he'd be eating reheated dinner after his kids were in bed.

"I want to believe this can work," Doug said quietly. "But I'm so tired. The whole team is tired. I don't know if we have the energy to transform anything. We're barely keeping the lights on."

Maya was quiet for a moment. "Can I tell you a story?"

"Sure."

"At my last company, we were in a similar situation. Different specifics, same fundamental problem. We were working eighty-hour weeks, shipping quarterly instead of weekly, losing talented people to burnout. I had one engineer—brilliant guy, been with us from day one—tell me he was going to quit because he couldn't take it anymore."

"What did you do?"

"I asked him to give me sixty days. Not sixty days of working harder—sixty days of working differently. I told him we were going to run an experiment with his team. New tools, new processes, new mindset. If it didn't work, I'd personally write him a recommendation letter."

"Did it work?"

"After thirty days, his team was deploying four times as often with fewer bugs. After sixty days, he told me he remembered why he loved engineering. Not because the technology changed—the technology was always there. But because we'd removed the obstacles that prevented him from using it effectively."

Doug wanted to believe it. He really did. But he'd seen too many transformation initiatives fail, too many consultants promise change and deliver PowerPoints.

"Why are you telling me this?" Doug asked.

"Because I see that same engineer in you," Maya said. "Someone who got into this field because they love building things, but who's been ground down by a system that makes building almost impossible. And I think you're in a position to change that. Not just for yourself—for your whole team."

"I'm not the CEO. I'm not even a VP. I can't just decree that we're going to work differently."

"No," Maya agreed. "But you can start. With your teams. With your sphere of influence. That's how transformation happens—not top-down mandates, but bottom-up proof."

Doug's phone buzzed again. "I need to get home."

"Of course." Maya opened the door, then paused. "Doug, I'm going to be working with the leadership team over the next few weeks, developing recommendations. But I'd like to work with you too. Not as a subject—as a partner. Someone who's willing to experiment, to try new approaches, to document what works and what doesn't."

"Why me?"

"Because you care," Maya said simply. "About your team, about your work, about building things that matter. People like you are the ones who change organizations."

"I'll think about it," Doug said.

"Fair enough." Maya closed the door and walked to her own car.

Doug sat for another minute, engine still off, staring at the office building. Sixty days, Maya had said. Her engineer had given her sixty days.

Could he do that? Could he commit to trying something different, even when he barely had the energy to get through the current week?

His phone buzzed a third time. Not Michelle—Sarah.

"Need you to review the incident post-mortem before the board meeting Monday. Tom wants engineering's perspective on the action items."

Doug closed his eyes. The board meeting. The ninety-day countdown. The constant pressure to fix everything immediately while also doing everything they'd always done.

He started the car and headed home, but Maya's question stayed with him:

What if the problem isn't your people, but your system?

And more importantly: what if there was a way to change the system without burning everyone out in the process?

Doug didn't have answers yet. But for the first time in months, he had something more valuable:

He had a question worth pursuing.
