# Chapter 16: The Crisis of Growth

Sarah called an emergency leadership meeting for 8 AM on a Tuesday morning. Doug arrived to find the conference room already full—Sarah, Tom, Mark, Keisha, Raj, Jen, and several other managers.

"Thank you for coming on short notice," Sarah said. She looked tired, which was unusual for her these days. "We have a problem."

She pulled up a slide showing their hiring pipeline. "Over the past three months, we've had unprecedented success. Our transformation is working. Customer metrics are strong. The board approved Series C funding. We're growing fast."

"That sounds like good news," Keisha said cautiously.

"It is good news. But growth brings new challenges." Sarah clicked to the next slide showing job requisitions. "Tom has approved thirty new engineering positions. We need to roughly double the size of the engineering organization in the next six months to support our roadmap."

Doug felt his stomach sink. Hiring was always hard, but doubling the team?

"That's not all," Sarah continued. "Recruiting is struggling. We've been interviewing candidates for the past month, and we have a problem: our interview process is designed for the old way of working. We're assessing people on skills and practices we don't use anymore."

She showed an example interview scorecard. "We test for ability to write code in a whiteboard setting. We ask about experience with our legacy tech stack. We evaluate based on years of experience with specific technologies."

"But none of that matters anymore," Raj said, understanding dawning. "What matters is whether someone can work effectively with AI tools, whether they think in terms of flow and systems, whether they can learn and adapt quickly."

"Exactly," Sarah said. "We're screening out great candidates who would thrive in our new environment, and we're hiring people who expect the old environment and struggle when they get here."

"How bad is it?" Doug asked.

Sarah pulled up the data. "Of the eight engineers we've hired in the past two months, three are thriving, two are struggling to adapt, and three are actively unhappy. One already resigned. Our new hire attrition is 37% within three months. That's disastrous."

Tom spoke up. "This is costing us money and momentum. Every failed hire sets us back months. We need to fix our hiring process, and we need to do it fast."

"What do the new hires say when they leave?" Keisha asked.

Sarah pulled up exit interview notes. "The common themes are: 'The job isn't what I expected.' 'I don't feel like my experience is valued.' 'Everything relies on AI and I'm not comfortable with that.' 'The pace of change is overwhelming.'"

Doug read through the comments. They painted a picture of culture shock—people expecting a traditional software job and finding something completely different.

"We need to rethink the entire hiring pipeline," Doug said. "Job descriptions, screening, interviews, onboarding. Everything."

"I agree," Sarah said. "That's why we're here. I need you all to figure this out. Doug, you lead this effort. Pull in whoever you need. We have thirty positions to fill, and we need to fill them with people who will succeed here."

---

Doug assembled a working group—Keisha, Lisa, Mike Chen (who'd become a valuable critic-turned-contributor), and Jennifer Lee. They spent two days analyzing the problem.

"The fundamental issue," Doug said, sketching on the whiteboard, "is that our culture has changed faster than our hiring practices. We're a different company than we were six months ago, but we're still hiring like the old company."

"So what defines the new company?" Jennifer asked.

They brainstormed a list:
- AI-first development practices
- Emphasis on flow and systems thinking
- Rapid iteration and continuous learning
- Minimal process, maximum autonomy
- Data-driven decision making
- Customer-centric product development

"This is very different from a traditional tech company," Mike observed. "Some people will love this. Others will hate it."

"And that's okay," Doug said. "We don't need to hire everyone. We need to hire people who will thrive here. The question is: how do we identify those people?"

Lisa pulled up her laptop. "I've been analyzing the engineers who are succeeding versus those who are struggling. I used AI to find patterns in their backgrounds, interview responses, and early performance."

She showed a analysis. "The successful hires share these traits: curiosity about new tools, comfort with ambiguity, growth mindset, collaborative work style, and focus on outcomes over process. Experience level doesn't matter—we have successful junior hires and successful senior hires. What matters is mindset and adaptability."

"The struggling hires tend to be more rigid," she continued. "They want clear processes, they resist new tools, they focus on individual tasks rather than team outcomes. They're not bad engineers—they're just not a good fit for our culture."

"So we need to screen for mindset, not just skills," Keisha said.

"Right. But how?" Jennifer asked. "You can't assess mindset in a resume."

Doug thought about the problem. "What if we changed the interview process entirely? Instead of whiteboard coding and technical trivia, what if we do a realistic work sample? Give candidates a small project that uses our actual tools and practices. See how they approach it."

"Like a paid trial project?" Mike asked.

"Exactly. A one-week consulting project. We pay them contractor rates to solve a real problem using our AI tools and workflows. At the end of the week, we see how they worked, what they built, how they adapted to the tools."

Keisha nodded slowly. "That would give us way more signal than a four-hour interview loop."

"But it's a huge time investment," Jennifer cautioned. "Both for us and for candidates."

"It's less time than hiring someone who quits after three months," Doug countered. "And it's a better experience for candidates—they get to see what the job is actually like before committing."

Lisa added, "We could even turn the project into an onboarding experience. If we hire them, they've already learned our tools and workflows. They can hit the ground running."

They refined the idea over several hours, designing a trial project process:

1. **Initial screening**: Resume review and brief phone screen to assess basic qualifications and interest in AI-native development.

2. **Trial project**: One-week paid engagement ($2,500) working on a real project with AI tools. Candidates get access to the codebase, tools, and can ask questions of the team.

3. **Evaluation**: Assess not just the output, but the process—how they used AI tools, how they asked questions, how they iterated, how they communicated.

4. **Mutual decision**: Both the company and candidate decide if there's a fit.

"This is radically different from standard tech hiring," Mike said. "Will candidates go for it?"

"The right candidates will," Doug said. "People who are curious about our approach, who want to try before they buy. People who are confident in their ability to learn."

"What about the job descriptions?" Keisha asked. "We should update those too."

They spent the afternoon rewriting the job descriptions to be honest about the culture:

*"At ShopStream, we build software differently. We leverage agentic AI to amplify human capability. We optimize for flow, not process. We ship multiple times a day. We measure everything and learn continuously. If you're curious about AI-native development, comfortable with rapid change, and excited about building the future of e-commerce, we want to talk to you."*

They included a section on what makes someone successful at ShopStream:
- Curiosity and willingness to learn new tools
- Systems thinking and focus on outcomes
- Comfort with AI collaboration
- Adaptability and growth mindset
- Strong communication and teamwork

They also listed what might make someone unsuccessful:
- Preference for rigid processes and procedures
- Resistance to new tools and practices
- Need for detailed specifications before starting work
- Discomfort with AI assistance
- Focus on individual work over collaboration

"This will turn some people off," Jennifer said, reading the draft.

"Good," Doug said. "We want self-selection. Better to have people opt out early than quit after three months."

---

They piloted the new hiring process with five candidates for backend engineering roles.

The first candidate, Ahmed, was a senior engineer with fifteen years of experience at traditional companies. He excelled in the initial phone screen, demonstrating deep technical knowledge. But during the trial week, he struggled with the AI tools and expressed frustration that there weren't detailed specifications for the project.

"I don't know what you want me to build," he said in the mid-week check-in. "Can someone write a requirements document?"

"The requirement is to solve the problem," Doug explained. "How you solve it is up to you. The AI can help generate options. You decide which approach makes sense."

Ahmed looked uncomfortable. "I'm used to more structure."

At the end of the week, it was clear he wasn't a fit. Ahmed agreed, saying, "This is too ambiguous for me. I need more defined processes."

It was a mutual parting, but Doug counted it as a success—they'd discovered the mismatch in a week rather than after three months of employment.

The second candidate, Priya, was a mid-level engineer with five years of experience. She took to the AI tools immediately, asking smart questions about how to best leverage them. Her trial project wasn't perfect, but her approach was impressive—she iterated quickly, asked for feedback early, and showed clear progress each day.

"This is amazing," she said in her final presentation. "I got more done in a week than I usually do in a month. The AI tools handle the boring stuff, so I can focus on design and problem-solving."

Priya was an immediate hire.

The third candidate, Jason, had only two years of experience but demonstrated exceptional adaptability. He'd never used AI coding tools before, but he learned them during the trial week and used them effectively. His project was creative and well-executed.

"Can I ask a question?" Jason said during his final interview. "Will I get to keep working like this if I join? Because this is the most fun I've had coding in my career."

"Yes," Doug said. "This is how we work. Every day."

Jason accepted the offer immediately.

The fourth and fifth candidates were similar stories—one was a great fit, one wasn't. But in every case, both the company and the candidate knew the outcome before making a commitment.

---

Doug presented the trial program results to Sarah and Tom after the first month.

"Of the twenty candidates who've gone through the trial process, twelve have been hired, and all twelve are thriving. Zero attrition so far. That's compared to our previous 37% attrition rate."

"What about time-to-hire?" Tom asked.

"It's longer—about three weeks per hire instead of two. But we're getting much better quality. And the candidates who join are already productive on day one because they've learned our tools and workflows."

Sarah looked at the data. "What's the cost difference?"

"The trial pay adds about $2,500 per hire," Doug calculated. "But we're saving on recruiter time—the trial is a better filter than multiple interview rounds. And we're saving massively on failed hires. Each failed hire costs us about $50,000 in recruiting, onboarding, and lost productivity. We've prevented at least five failed hires so far, so we're net positive $225,000."

"I like this," Tom said. "But can it scale? We need to fill thirty positions."

"We've refined the process," Doug said. "We can run multiple trial projects in parallel. Keisha's team is running one, Jennifer's team is running one, Raj is mentoring one. We can probably evaluate ten candidates per month across the organization."

"So three months to fill all thirty positions," Sarah calculated.

"Approximately. Some roles will take longer if we don't find the right fit quickly. But I'd rather take four months to hire the right people than two months to hire people who'll quit."

Tom nodded. "Agreed. Make this the standard hiring process."

---

Over the next three months, the hiring transformation took hold.

The new job descriptions attracted different candidates—fewer "I have ten years of experience with Java" applicants, more "I'm fascinated by AI-native development and want to learn" applicants.

The trial projects became a factory for building culture. Candidates who succeeded in the trial understood the company's approach and were excited about it. Candidates who struggled learned it wasn't the right fit before making a career move.

By the end of the quarter, they'd hired twenty-three of the thirty positions—a remarkable achievement given the selectiveness of the process.

But more importantly, the new hires were contributing from day one. They didn't need weeks of onboarding or culture adjustment. They'd already lived the culture during their trial.

One of the new hires, Priya, became a champion for the approach. In an all-hands meeting, she shared her experience.

"I've been here two months," she said. "But I feel like I've been here much longer because of the trial project. I knew what I was getting into. I knew how we worked. I knew the tools. There were no surprises. And honestly, this is the best job I've ever had. The autonomy, the tools, the pace of learning—it's incredible."

After the meeting, Maya caught up with Doug.

"The hiring transformation was essential," she said. "You can't scale a new culture by hiring people from the old culture and expecting them to change."

"We learned that the hard way," Doug admitted.

"But you learned it and adapted quickly. That's the mark of a learning organization. Most companies would have kept hiring the same way and wondered why the new hires kept failing."

"We almost did that," Doug said. "If Sarah hadn't called it out, we might have kept going."

"Leadership is about seeing problems before they become crises," Maya said. "Sarah saw it. You solved it. That's how transformation succeeds—by continuously identifying and addressing obstacles."

---

That evening, Doug reflected on the hiring journey with Michelle.

"We almost derailed the entire transformation," he told her. "We were growing fast, hiring aggressively, and bringing in people who didn't fit. It could have poisoned the culture."

"But you caught it," Michelle said.

"We caught it. And we fixed it. But it's a reminder that transformation isn't a one-time thing. Every time we solve one challenge, a new one emerges."

"That sounds exhausting."

Doug smiled. "It is. But it's also energizing. We're building something that keeps evolving, keeps improving. It never gets boring."

"I'm glad you're not as stressed as you were six months ago," Michelle said. "Even with all these challenges, you seem... lighter."

Doug thought about that. She was right. Despite the challenges—the scaling issues, the hiring crisis, the constant adaptation—he felt more in control now than he had when things were simpler but more chaotic.

"I think it's because we have systems now," he explained. "When problems come up, we have a way to solve them. We measure, we experiment, we learn, we adapt. It's not personal stress anymore—it's just problems to solve."

"And you have a team," Michelle added. "You're not doing this alone."

"Yeah. That makes a huge difference."

Later, lying in bed, Doug thought about the crisis of growth they'd just navigated.

Six months ago, the crisis was survival—how to avoid shutdown.

Three months ago, the crisis was transformation—how to change while keeping the ship afloat.

Now, the crisis was growth—how to scale while preserving culture.

Each crisis was different, but the approach was the same: identify the problem, understand it deeply, experiment with solutions, measure results, adapt.

They'd become a learning organization, capable of tackling whatever challenges emerged.

And Doug suspected there would be many more challenges ahead.

But for the first time, he felt ready for them.
